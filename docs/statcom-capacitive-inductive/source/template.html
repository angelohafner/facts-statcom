STEP 01 / FUNDAMENTAL IDEA

Two voltages. One reactor. One current.

Why does a difference between converter and grid voltage create reactive current?

01
Equivalent circuit · fundamental-frequency modelBalanced · ideal

The left source is the fundamental AC voltage equivalent of the VSC—not the DC capacitor voltage or instantaneous PWM waveform.

Observe the cause

A STATCOM produces an AC voltage E. The PCC has an AC voltage V. Their phasor difference appears across the coupling reactance X.

Dividing by j rotates the voltage-difference phasor by −90°. That rotation explains the reactive character of the current.

E > VCapacitive
E = VZero exchange
E < VInductive

KEY IDEA Changing the converter's fundamental voltage changes the current through the reactor.

Check your intuition: If E and V are identical phasors, what voltage remains across the reactor?

STEP 02 / INJECTION

Higher converter voltage → capacitive support

Why does the STATCOM supply reactive power when E is greater than V?

CAPACITIVE · Q > 0
CONVERTERE = 1.10 pu
PCCV = 1.00 pu
REACTANCEX = 0.20 pu
REACTIVE POWERQinj = +0.50 pu
Higher E→Positive E − V→Downward Iinj phasor→Reactive support

Current reference matters: these figures define injected current from converter to PCC. Its −90° angle is correct for this reference. Reversing the current reference also reverses the current phasor.

STEP 03 / ABSORPTION

Lower converter voltage → inductive absorption

What changes if the converter's fundamental voltage falls below the PCC voltage?

INDUCTIVE · Q < 0
CONVERTERE = 0.90 pu
PCCV = 1.00 pu
REACTANCEX = 0.20 pu
REACTIVE POWERQinj = −0.50 pu
Lower E→Negative E − V→Upward Iinj phasor→Reactive absorption

Only one input changed: E dropped from 1.10 to 0.90 pu. The PCC voltage, coupling reactance, phase angle, and current reference remain the same.
